Chris Voss was the FBI’s lead hostage negotiator. His book adapts tactics forged in life-or-death situations for everyday negotiations—whether regarding salaries, team conflicts, or family matters.

The core premise: many negotiation models fail because they treat people as rational, when in reality, they are emotional, impulsive, and driven by fear. Negotiation is not a battle; it is a **process of discovery**—the goal is to uncover as much information as possible.

---

## Tactical Empathy

Tactical empathy means understanding the other person’s feelings and mindset in the moment, as well as listening for what lies beneath those emotions.

It is not about agreeing with the other person or being soft; it is about demonstrating that you grasp their perspective. This builds trust and lowers defenses.

> A person who feels secure, understood, and heard will be more open to listening and sharing freely.

The beauty of empathy is that it does not require you to agree with the other person’s ideas.

---

## Mirroring

Repeat the last 1–3 words (or the key words) of what the other person just said, using a calm tone and an upward inflection. Then, remain silent.

This encourages the other person to elaborate on what they said, establishes a rhythm, makes them feel heard, and buys you time to think.

> Mirroring—such as saying, "Help me understand"—signals that you are neither defensive nor looking for a fight.

The intent behind mirroring is never to attack; rather, it is an invitation for the other person to expand on their thoughts. By mirroring, you help the other person process their own problem, which leads them to reveal more information.

---

## Labeling

Labeling involves naming emotions or perspectives to demonstrate understanding—even when you aren't entirely certain. Labels begin with:

- "It seems like..."
- "It sounds like..."
- "It looks like..."

Then, pause and let it do its work.

Labeling helps a person feel validated and calms their emotional brain. It stops negative emotions before they escalate.

The best way to deal with negativity is to observe it—without reacting and without judging.

### Accusation Audit

An "accusation audit" involves listing the potential negative points or accusations the other person might hold against you *before* they bring them up. This defuses negativity and gives you control over the conversation.

## Beware of "Yes" — Master "No"

Contrary to most advice, "No" is not a rejection; it is a form of **protection**. When people say "no," they feel secure, in control, and under less pressure.

> Saying "no" often brings us closer to an agreement than a "yes" ever could.

"No" is a reaffirmation of autonomy; it opens up the discussion. The sooner it is said, the sooner the other person becomes open to considering options they hadn't seen before.

A premature "yes" is often a way to dodge the issue—"you're right" can be a polite way to shut down a conversation without making any real commitment.

A good question to elicit a "no" is: "Have you abandoned this project?"

---

## "That's right" vs. "You're right"

The moment someone says, "That's right," is the moment they feel truly understood and become open to alignment.

Avoid saying, "You're right"—this is often just a polite way to end a conversation.

How to achieve this: summarize their story, their challenges, and their point of view by combining paraphrasing with labeling. When your counterpart says "that’s right," they feel that they have evaluated what you said and have stated it of their own free will.

## Calibrated Questions

Open-ended questions that begin with **"How"** and **"What"**: they are less threatening, avoid yes/no answers, and keep the other party psychologically engaged.

- "What about this is important to you?"
- "How would you like us to proceed?"
- "What is the biggest challenge you see here?"
- "How am I supposed to do that?"

Calibrated questions create the **illusion of control**: you get the other person to solve your problem while making them feel that the solution was their own idea. This is why negotiation is called "the art of letting someone else have your way."

**Bad question:** "Why haven't you approved this yet?"
**Better question:** "What is the biggest obstacle to moving forward with this?"

Questions to reveal the thinking of committees and teams:

- "How does this affect the rest of your team?"
- "To what extent are the people not on this call on board?"

When you are verbally attacked, do not counterattack; instead, disarm your counterpart with a calibrated question.

## Ensuring Execution

A deal is not closed until it is executed.

### The 7-38-55 Rule

When someone’s tone of voice or body language does not align with the words they are speaking, use labels to uncover the source of the inconsistency.

> You: "So, are we in agreement?"
> Them: "Yes..."
> You: "I heard you say 'yes,' but it sounded like there was some doubt in your voice."

### The Rule of Three

Getting the other party to agree to the same thing **three times**
